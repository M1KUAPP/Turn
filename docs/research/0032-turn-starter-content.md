## How validated pain tools ask about pain

- **Short-form McGill Pain Questionnaire (SF-MPQ).** Its main part "consists
  of 15 descriptors (11 sensory; 4 affective) which are rated on an
  intensity scale as 0 = none, 1 = mild, 2 = moderate or 3 = severe". It
  also keeps "the Present Pain Intensity (PPI) index of the standard MPQ and
  a visual analogue scale (VAS)" ([Melzack 1987][sf-mpq], abstract read
  through Europe PMC).
- **SF-MPQ words not read.** The abstract doesn't list the 15 descriptors,
  and the questionnaire itself wasn't fetched, so this note gives no pain
  quality words. See [Gaps](#gaps).
- **Brief Pain Inventory (BPI).** MD Anderson, which owns it, says "The BPI
  rapidly assesses the severity of pain and its impact on functioning." Its
  page lists four severity items (worst, least, average, and right now) and
  seven interference items: general activity, mood, walking ability, work,
  relations, sleep, and enjoyment of life ([BPI][bpi]).
- **Scale labels and body map not read.** The BPI page shows the form only
  as images, and its user guide's address returned HTTP 404, so the 0 to 10
  scale's end labels and the body-diagram instruction are unverified.
- Synthesis: pain phrases can name severity in the SF-MPQ's own words,
  "mild", "moderate", and "severe", which a partner can map to a scale, plus
  "No pain" for the scale's 0.
- Synthesis: the BPI's time frames suggest phrases a partner can act on,
  such as "It hurts right now", "It's worse than before", and "This is the
  worst it's been".
- Synthesis: the BPI's interference items suggest a few phrases about
  effects, such as "The pain keeps me awake" and "It hurts to walk".
- Synthesis: Turn is a text app, so a 0 to 10 number can be typed; one
  phrase such as "I'll type my pain as a number from 0 to 10" may beat ten
  number phrases. Ask the campus clinic (CONTENT-5).

[sf-mpq]: https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3959(87)91074-8
[bpi]: https://www.mdanderson.org/research/departments-labs-institutes/departments-divisions/symptom-research/symptom-assessment-tools/brief-pain-inventory.html

## Consent and refusal for people who can't speak

- **Communication Bill of Rights.** The National Joint Committee (NJC)
  publishes it on ASHA's site, now in its 3rd edition. Its rights include
  "The right to ask for or refuse objects, actions, events, and people" and
  "The right to make choices from meaningful options" ([NJC][njc]).
- **Being told and being answered.** It also lists "The right to be
  given—and to understand—information about objects, actions, events, and
  people" and "The right to receive a response to all communication, even
  when the desired outcome is not possible" ([NJC][njc]).
- **US access law not read.** The HHS Office for Civil Rights page on
  effective communication refused the fetch (HTTP 403). See [Gaps](#gaps).
- Synthesis: the care phrases should let the user refuse ("No, I don't want
  that", "Stop, please"), agree ("Yes, go ahead"), and ask for something,
  matching the right to "ask for or refuse".
- Synthesis: the right to understand calls for phrases that pause care until
  the user follows it: "Wait", "Please explain that again", "What are you
  going to do?", and "Ask me before you touch me".
- Synthesis: the right to a response calls for phrases like "Please answer
  me" and "I'm not finished"; check overlap with the strip's "Wait, I'm
  typing" first.
- Synthesis: the NJC's text is a rights statement, not clinical consent
  guidance, so treat these phrases as a floor for the campus clinic to
  review, not as a legal consent standard.

[njc]: https://www.asha.org/njc/communication-bill-of-rights/

## Default content in text AAC apps

- **TD Snap Text phrases.** The Phrases pages hold "prestored messages that
  are organized into categories", and "General categories and a few sample
  messages have been provided. Modify and add your own categories and
  messages to suit your needs." They are "commonly used messages for
  everyday environments" ([TD Snap Text cards][snap-cards]).
- **TD Snap Text QuickFires.** QuickFires "Produce interjections in any
  topic and environment" ([TD Snap Text cards][snap-cards]).
- **TD Snap Text practice.** Users should "Think of specific situations and
  use a role playing activity to practice using phrases", and "If an
  important category is missing, you can add your own by modifying one of
  the custom topics" ([TD Snap Text cards][snap-cards]).
- **Proloquo4Text.** "Quick Blocks give rapid access to customizable
  categories of Phrases (for commonly used phrases), Quick Talk (for quick
  interjections), Conversations (to easily switch between conversations)"
  ([Proloquo4Text 4.0 manual][p4t-manual]).
- **Speech Assistant AAC.** "The first time the app is started, it creates a
  set of default categories and phrases in the language of your iPhone or
  iPad (English, French, German or Dutch)", and "You can modify or delete
  phrases by long pressing a phrase button or in the settings"
  ([Speech Assistant AAC manual][sa-manual]).
- **Category names not found.** None of the three documents names its
  default categories or lists its sample phrases in the text read.
- Synthesis: all three ship a small, general seed that the user is expected
  to edit, so Turn's bank can stay at 140 to 160 phrases and make editing
  obvious rather than try to be complete.
- Synthesis: TD Snap Text and Proloquo4Text keep quick interjections apart
  from phrase categories (QuickFires, Quick Talk), as Turn's Quick category
  and strip do.
- Synthesis: TD Snap Text's "everyday environments" and role-play advice fit
  Turn's four places; a team review could role-play one visit to Home,
  Clinic, Shop, and Out and note missing phrases.

[p4t-manual]: https://download.assistiveware.com/proloquo4text/files/Proloquo4Text-4.0-manual-EN.pdf
[sa-manual]: https://www.asoft.nl/SpeechAssistantAAC-iOS-UserManual.pdf

## Plain-language rules for short phrases

- **Where the federal guidance lives now.** Digital.gov says "Selections of
  PlainLanguage.gov content–especially those most relevant for digital
  teams–have been carried forward in this set of plain language guides"
  ([plain language guides][pl-guides]).
- **One idea each.** Under "Write short sentences": "Express only one idea
  in each sentence." It adds "Long, complicated sentences often mean that
  you aren't sure about what you want to say" ([clear and short][pl-short]).
- **Familiar words.** "Using words that readers know helps them to
  understand", so "pick the familiar or commonly used word over the unusual
  or obscure". Its swaps include "aid, help" for "assist, assistance",
  "begin, start" for "commence", and "use" for "utilize, utilization"
  ([familiar terms][pl-terms]).
- **Active voice.** "Active voice makes it clear who should do what"
  ([writing][pl-writing]).
- **No word count found.** The pages read give no sentence-length limit in
  words.
- Synthesis: each phrase should carry one idea, in the first person and
  active voice ("I need to use the bathroom"), so a partner knows who needs
  what.
- Synthesis: prefer the everyday word ("help", "stop", "start", "hurt") over
  the clinical one ("assist", "discomfort").
- Synthesis: the 120-character cap is a ceiling; most phrases should be one
  short sentence.

[pl-guides]: https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/
[pl-short]: https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing/clear-short
[pl-terms]: https://digital.gov/guides/writing-understanding/familiar-terms
[pl-writing]: https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing
